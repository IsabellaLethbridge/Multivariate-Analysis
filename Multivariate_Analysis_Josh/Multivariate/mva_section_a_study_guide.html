must
Sample covariance matrix — know how to write it (page 4). Stefan: "It's something you maybe want to spend a little bit of time on. It's probing." Know the matrix form: \((n-1)\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X} - n\bar{\mathbf{x}}\bar{\mathbf{x}}' = \mathbf{X}'\!\left(\mathbf{I}-\tfrac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}'\right)\!\mathbf{X}\). He confirmed a past exam question asked students to write this fully — and it was not well answered.
must
Deviation vectors — page 9, point 3 is the most salient point. Stefan: "How can we relate the deviation vectors back to the angle between vectors? How does that relate to the correlation?" Know the three-bullet summary:
  • Projection of \(\mathbf{x}_i\) onto \(\mathbf{1}\) gives \(\bar{x}_i\); length \(\sqrt{n}|\bar{x}_i|\).
  • \(\mathbf{d}_i'\mathbf{d}_i = (n-1)s_{ii}\) and \(\mathbf{d}_i'\mathbf{d}_k = (n-1)s_{ik}\).
  • Point 3 (most salient): \(r_{ik} = \cos(\theta)\) where \(\theta\) is the angle between \(\mathbf{d}_i\) and \(\mathbf{d}_k\).
"I'm not going to ask you to draw anything." The geometry is conceptual.

must
Know the three decompositions conceptually. Eigendecomposition \(\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{U}'\), spectral decomposition \(\mathbf{A}=\sum\lambda_i\mathbf{e}_i\mathbf{e}_i'\) (including inverse and square-root forms), and SVD \(\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{V}'=\sum\sqrt{\lambda_i}\mathbf{u}_i\mathbf{v}_i'\). These are used throughout (PCA, CCA) so must be solid.
proof
§2.3 — Prove that the rank-\(s\) SVD truncation is the best low-rank approximation (least-squares sense). Stefan confirmed this is a proof when asked directly. He also confirmed it is "something you should know" and that "sometimes rote learning is preferable — it is unavoidable." Stefan: "I wouldn't even say it's unavoidable. It is preferable."

Proof minimises \(\text{tr}[(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B})(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B})']\):
  • Insert \(\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}'=\mathbf{I}\) and \(\mathbf{V}\mathbf{V}'=\mathbf{I}\): reduces to \(\text{tr}[(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}-\mathbf{C})(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}-\mathbf{C})']\) with \(\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{U}'\mathbf{B}\mathbf{V}\).
  • Expand: \(\sum_{i,j}(\sqrt{\lambda_{ij}}-c_{ij})^2 = \sum_i(\sqrt{\lambda_i}-c_{ii})^2 + \sum_{i\neq j}c_{ij}^2\).
  • Minimised when off-diagonals \(c_{ij}=0\) and \(c_{ii}=\sqrt{\lambda_i}\) for the \(s\) largest, zero otherwise.
  • Hence \(\mathbf{U}'\mathbf{B}\mathbf{V}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_s\), giving \(\mathbf{B}=\sum_{i=1}^s\sqrt{\lambda_i}\mathbf{u}_i\mathbf{v}_i'\). \(\square\)

Q1 · 2 marks
First question in the exam: write the PDF of the multivariate normal distribution. Stefan: "If you don't get two out of two for that one I really sit there for a minute." Do not lose these marks. \[f(\mathbf{x}) = (2\pi)^{-p/2}|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}|^{-1/2}\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})\right), \quad \mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^p\] Know every part: normalising constant, the generalised distance in the exponent, and write \(\mathbf{X}\sim N_p(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\).
must
Constant density contours and ellipsoid geometry (§3.2.2). "Constant density contours — this is quite important conceptually." Contours are ellipsoids \(\{(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})=c^2\}\). Axes in directions of eigenvectors of \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) with half-lengths \(c\sqrt{\lambda_i}\). Bivariate case: when \(\sigma_{11}=\sigma_{22}\), eigenvalues are \(\lambda_1=\sigma_{11}+\sigma_{12}\), \(\lambda_2=\sigma_{11}-\sigma_{12}\), major axis at 45°.
proof
§3.3 — Show that the generalised distance is \(\chi^2_p\) distributed (Homework Exercise 3.2). Stefan: "This is the one exception for the continuous assessments — absolutely critical to be able to show that." When asked if they could use either method (spectral decomp or another), he said he might ask for a specific one. Learn the spectral decomposition proof:
  • Write \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{P}'\). Let \(\mathbf{Y}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1/2}\mathbf{P}'(\mathbf{X}-\boldsymbol{\mu})\).
  • \(\text{Cov}(\mathbf{Y})=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1/2}\mathbf{P}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1/2}=\mathbf{I}_p\), so \(\mathbf{Y}\sim N_p(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})\).
  • \(\mathbf{Y}'\mathbf{Y}=\sum Y_i^2\sim\chi^2_p\). But \(\mathbf{Y}'\mathbf{Y}=(\mathbf{X}-\boldsymbol{\mu})'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{X}-\boldsymbol{\mu})\). \(\square\)
Consequence: \(P[(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})\leq\chi^2_p(\alpha)]=1-\alpha\).
must
Properties §3.4.1–3.4.3 — go through them, used throughout. Stefan: "Absolutely you should go through them, but we went through them first so we could use them later."
  • Linear combinations normal: \(\mathbf{a}'\mathbf{X}\sim N(\mathbf{a}'\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathbf{a}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{a})\); \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}\sim N_q(\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{A}')\).
  • Subsets are MVN: \(\mathbf{X}_1\sim N_q(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11})\).
  • Zero cov \(\Leftrightarrow\) independence in MVN only: \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}=\mathbf{0}\Leftrightarrow\mathbf{X}_1\perp\!\!\!\perp\mathbf{X}_2\).
"A linear combination of normals is normal. Just add the parameters in that way. Nothing funny there."
proof
§3.4.4 — Conditional distribution of \(\mathbf{X}_1|\mathbf{X}_2\). Stefan: "This is another thing we should show... going through this is a worthwhile exercise."
Key point from Stefan: "I would never expect you to go and memorise some seemingly arbitrary matrix for the purposes of proving a specific result. I would always tell you: using \(\mathbf{A}\) defined as such, derive the distribution of \(\mathbf{X}_1\) given \(\mathbf{X}_2=\mathbf{x}_2\)." So the matrix will be given — know how to use it.

The transformation \(\mathbf{A}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}_q & -\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1}\\ \mathbf{0} & \mathbf{I}_{p-q}\end{pmatrix}\) gives \(\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{A}'=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21}&\mathbf{0}\\\mathbf{0}&\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}\end{pmatrix}\). Off-diagonal \(\mathbf{0}\Rightarrow\) independence \(\Rightarrow\) derive conditional. Result: \[\mathbf{X}_1|\mathbf{X}_2=\mathbf{x}_2\sim N_q\!\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}_2-\boldsymbol{\mu}_2),\;\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21}\right)\] Bivariate simplification: \(X_1|X_2\sim N\!\left(\mu_1+\frac{\sigma_{12}}{\sigma_{22}}(x_2-\mu_2),\;\sigma_{11}-\frac{\sigma_{12}^2}{\sigma_{22}}\right)\).
NB
§3.5 — Linear combinations of random vectors. Stefan: "3.5 is going to be important." If \(\mathbf{X}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{X}_n\) independent with \(\mathbf{X}_j\sim N_p(\boldsymbol{\mu}_j,\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\): \(\sum c_j\mathbf{X}_j\sim N_p(\sum c_j\boldsymbol{\mu}_j,(\sum c_j^2)\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\). Independence of \(\mathbf{V}_1=\sum c_j\mathbf{X}_j\) and \(\mathbf{V}_2=\sum b_j\mathbf{X}_j\) when \(\mathbf{b}'\mathbf{c}=0\). Also look at exercise 3.6.

NB
Theorems A.1–A.4 in Appendix A will be given in the exam. Stefan confirmed this emphatically. You do not need to memorise or prove these — but you must know how to apply them. Theorem A.1: \(\partial(\mathbf{x}'\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x})/\partial\mathbf{x}=2\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\). A.2: \(\partial\log|\mathbf{A}|/\partial\mathbf{A}=(\mathbf{A}^{-1})'\). A.3: \(\partial\text{tr}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})/\partial\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{A}'\). A.4: block determinant identity (used in Ch 5).
must
Log-likelihood derivation — the summation identity. Key step: rewrite \(\sum(\mathbf{x}_j-\boldsymbol{\mu})'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}_j-\boldsymbol{\mu}) = \text{tr}[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{A}]+n(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mu})'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mu})\) where \(\mathbf{A}=(n-1)\mathbf{S}\). Uses the trace trick \(\mathbf{a}'\mathbf{B}\mathbf{a}=\text{tr}(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}')\). Stefan confirmed this result is used again in Ch 5.
proof
MLE of \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\): \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}=\bar{\mathbf{x}}\). Using Theorem A.1 (given): \(\partial\ell/\partial\boldsymbol{\mu}=n\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mu})=\mathbf{0}\Rightarrow\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}=\bar{\mathbf{x}}\). Second derivative \(-n\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\) is negative definite \(\Rightarrow\) maximum.
proof
MLE of \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\): \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}=\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{A}=\frac{n-1}{n}\mathbf{S}\). Using Theorems A.2 and A.3 (given): rewrite \(\ell\) using \(\log|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}|\), then \(\partial\ell/\partial\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}=\frac{n}{2}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{A}'=\mathbf{0}\Rightarrow\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}=\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{A}\).

Showing the MLE is a maximum not a saddle point (pages 37–38). Stefan explicitly: "After getting the MLE of sigma, the rest of page 37, skip. Page 38 up to where we finish saying it's indeed a maximum — skip." The reparametrisation via eigenvalues \(\lambda_i=n\) argument is NOT required.
NB
Bias of \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\) — explicitly flagged in both sources. \(E(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})=\frac{n-1}{n}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\neq\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\). The MLE is biased. Unbiased estimator is \(\mathbf{S}\) (denominator \(n-1\)).
NB
Maximised likelihood — substitute MLEs back in. Stefan: "Substituting in the MLEs to get the actual maximized likelihood — that is important in Chapter 5 later when we do the likelihood ratio stuff." Result: \[L(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}) = (2\pi)^{-np/2}\left(\tfrac{1}{n}\right)^{n/2}|\mathbf{A}|^{-n/2}e^{-np/2}\] This is used directly in the LRT numerator/denominator in §5.3.
must
Sampling distributions — Wishart basics. Stefan: "You must know at least the basics of the distribution of the sample covariance matrix." For i.i.d. \(N_p(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\) sample: \(\bar{\mathbf{X}}\sim N_p(\boldsymbol{\mu},\frac{1}{n}\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\), \((n-1)\mathbf{S}\sim W_p(n-1,\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\), \(\bar{\mathbf{X}}\perp\!\!\!\perp\mathbf{S}\). Wishart key property: \(W_1(n,\sigma^2)=\sigma^2\chi^2_n\). Large sample: \(n(\bar{\mathbf{X}}-\boldsymbol{\mu})'\mathbf{S}^{-1}(\bar{\mathbf{X}}-\boldsymbol{\mu})\dot{\sim}\chi^2_p\).

§5.2 — Hotelling's \(T^2\): everything. Stefan: "5.2, yes yes yes yes yes yes." Definition, distribution, rejection rule, example: \[T^2 = n(\bar{\mathbf{X}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_0)'\mathbf{S}^{-1}(\bar{\mathbf{X}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_0)\sim\frac{(n-1)p}{n-p}F_{p,n-p}\] Reject \(H_0:\boldsymbol{\mu}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_0\) when \(\frac{n-p}{(n-1)p}T^2>F_{p,n-p}(\alpha)\). §5.3 — Full LRT derivation: likelihood ratio, restricted MLE of \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\), Wilks' \(\Lambda\). Stefan: "You must be able to reproduce exactly what we did here in 5.3." Steps:
  • Define LRT: \(\Lambda = \max_{\Theta_0}L/\max_\Theta L\). Unrestricted maximum from Ch 4 is known.
  • Restricted MLE under \(H_0\): \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_0=\frac{1}{n}[\mathbf{A}+n(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_0)(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_0)']\). Derive this similarly to Ch 4 MLE.
  • Show \(\sum(\mathbf{x}_j-\boldsymbol{\mu}_0)'\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_0^{-1}(\mathbf{x}_j-\boldsymbol{\mu}_0)=np\) (so the exponential terms cancel).
  • Wilks' \(\Lambda^{2/n}=|\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}|/|\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_0|=|\mathbf{A}|/|\mathbf{A}+n(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_0)(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_0)'|\).
proof
§5.3.1 — Link between \(\Lambda\) and \(T^2\) using Theorem A.4 (given). Stefan: "A good relationship to be able to show. I would definitely say use the matrix B and this relationship." He also confirmed the bottom of page 51 (the two-determinants form) is needed.

Construct \(\mathbf{B}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{A}&\sqrt{n}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_0)\\\sqrt{n}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_0)'&-1\end{pmatrix}\), apply Theorem A.4 both ways: \[\frac{|\mathbf{A}|}{|\mathbf{A}+n(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_0)(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_0)'|}=\left(1+\frac{T^2}{n-1}\right)^{-1}\] Therefore: \(\Lambda^{2/n}=\left(1+\frac{T^2}{n-1}\right)^{-1}\).

Two-determinants form (bottom of p. 51 — Stefan confirmed this too): \[T^2 = \frac{(n-1)\left|\sum_{j=1}^n(\mathbf{x}_j-\boldsymbol{\mu}_0)(\mathbf{x}_j-\boldsymbol{\mu}_0)'\right|}{\left|\sum_{j=1}^n(\mathbf{x}_j-\bar{\mathbf{x}})(\mathbf{x}_j-\bar{\mathbf{x}})'\right|} - (n-1)\] This form computes \(T^2\) from two determinants without inverting \(\mathbf{S}\).
must
