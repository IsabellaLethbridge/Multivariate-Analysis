Running Example
Throughout this summary we use a single consistent example: we measure yield (tonnes/ha) and plant height (cm) on crops grown under three fertiliser treatments (\(g=3\) groups). In the two-way section we add a second factor: two irrigation levels (\(b=2\)). All interpretations refer back to this example. Our response vector is always \(\mathbf{X}_{lj} = (\text{yield},\ \text{height})'\) so \(p=2\).
1
ANOVA — The Univariate Foundation

ANOVA is the special case of MANOVA where we measure only one variable (\(p=1\)). Understanding it clearly makes the multivariate extension natural.

The Model

Each observation \(X_{lj}\) (the \(j\)th observation in group \(l\)) is decomposed as:

ANOVA Model
\[X_{lj} = \underbrace{\mu}_{\text{overall mean}} + \underbrace{\tau_l}_{\text{group effect}} + \underbrace{e_{lj}}_{\text{random error}}\] where \(e_{lj} \sim N(0, \sigma^2)\) and the constraint \(\sum_{l=1}^g n_l \tau_l = 0\) ensures uniqueness.

The constraint forces \(\mu\) to equal the weighted overall mean \(\bar{x}\), making the \(\tau_l\) deviations from that overall mean. Without it, infinitely many \((\mu, \tau_l)\) combinations would give identical fitted values.

Example — Yield only
If mean yield under fertilisers \(1, 2, 3\) is \(40, 50, 45\) tonnes/ha with equal group sizes \(n_l = 10\), then the overall mean is \(\bar{x} = 45\) and the treatment effects are \(\tau_1 = -5,\ \tau_2 = +5,\ \tau_3 = 0\). These sum to zero as required.
Partitioning Variation

The total deviation of any observation from the overall mean can be split into two parts:

Total \((X_{lj} - \bar{x})\)
=
Treatment (Between) \((\bar{x}_l - \bar{x})\)
+
Residual (Within) \((X_{lj} - \bar{x}_l)\)
Source Sum of Squares df Interpretation
Treatment (Between) \(\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^g n_l(\bar{x}_l - \bar{x})^2\) \(g-1\) How far each group mean is from the overall mean
Residual (Within) \(\displaystyle\sum_l\sum_j (x_{lj} - \bar{x}_l)^2\) \(\sum n_l - g\) How far observations are from their own group mean
Total \(\displaystyle\sum_l\sum_j (x_{lj} - \bar{x})^2\) \(\sum n_l - 1\) How far observations are from the overall mean

The degrees of freedom make intuitive sense: treatment loses 1 df for estimating \(\bar{x}\); residual loses \(g\) df, one per group mean; total loses just 1 df for \(\bar{x}\).

The F-Test
ANOVA F-statistic
\[F = \frac{SS_{tr}/(g-1)}{SS_{res}/(\sum n_l - g)} \sim F_{g-1,\, \sum n_l - g}\] Reject \(H_0: \tau_1 = \cdots = \tau_g = 0\) if \(F > F_{g-1,\, \sum n_l - g}(\alpha)\).

This is equivalent to a regression \(F\)-test with \(g-1\) dummy variables — ANOVA is just regression with a categorical predictor.

2
One-Way MANOVA

Now each observation is a vector of \(p\) variables measured simultaneously. In our example, each crop plot gives us a vector of yield and height: \(\mathbf{X}_{lj} = (\text{yield},\ \text{height})'\).

Assumptions
01
Independence
Samples from different groups are independent of each other.
02
Common covariance
All groups share the same \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) — verified using Box's M test.
03
Multivariate normality
Each group follows \(N_p(\boldsymbol{\mu}_l, \boldsymbol{\Sigma})\).
The Model
MANOVA Model
\[\mathbf{X}_{lj} = \boldsymbol{\mu} + \boldsymbol{\tau}_l + \mathbf{e}_{lj}, \quad j=1,\ldots,n_l,\ l=1,\ldots,g\] where \(\mathbf{e}_{lj} \sim N_p(\mathbf{0}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma})\) and \(\sum_{l=1}^g n_l \boldsymbol{\tau}_l = \mathbf{0}\).

The key extension: the \(p\) variables within each observation vector are correlated with each other — this correlation is captured in \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\). Observations across groups remain independent.

SSP Matrices — Replacing Sums of Squares

Instead of scalar sums of squares, we now have \(p \times p\) matrices of sums of squares and cross-products (SSP). The diagonal entries are the usual univariate SS for each variable; the off-diagonals capture covariation between variables.

Source SSP Matrix df
Treatment B \(\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^g n_l(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_l - \bar{\mathbf{x}})(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_l - \bar{\mathbf{x}})'\) \(g-1\)
Residual W \(\displaystyle\sum_l\sum_j (\mathbf{x}_{lj} - \bar{\mathbf{x}}_l)(\mathbf{x}_{lj} - \bar{\mathbf{x}}_l)'\) \(\sum n_l - g\)
Total \(\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{W}\) \(\sum n_l - 1\)
Example — computing W
For our two-variable example, \(\mathbf{W}\) is a \(2 \times 2\) matrix. For each observation, compute the outer product of its deviation from its group mean, then sum across all observations and groups: \[\mathbf{x}_{lj} - \bar{\mathbf{x}}_l = \begin{pmatrix}\text{yield}_{lj} - \bar{\text{yield}}_l \\ \text{height}_{lj} - \bar{\text{height}}_l\end{pmatrix}\] The diagonal of \(\mathbf{W}\) gives \(SS_{res}\) for yield and for height separately — exactly the ANOVA residual \(SS\) for each variable. The off-diagonal gives the cross-product, capturing how the two variables co-vary within groups.
3
Wilks' Lambda & Testing

In ANOVA we form \(F = B/W\). In MANOVA we cannot divide matrices, so instead we use determinants to compress each matrix to a single number (the generalised variance), giving Wilks' \(\Lambda^*\).

Wilks' Lambda — Three Equivalent Forms
\[\Lambda^* = \frac{|\mathbf{W}|}{|\mathbf{W}+\mathbf{B}|} = \prod_{i=1}^s \frac{1}{1+\lambda_i} = \frac{|\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_0|}{|\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}|}\] where \(s = \min(p, g-1)\) and \(\lambda_1 \geq \cdots \geq \lambda_s\) are eigenvalues of \(\mathbf{W}^{-1}\mathbf{B}\).
When \(\Lambda^* \approx 1\)
\(|\mathbf{W}| \approx |\mathbf{W}+\mathbf{B}|\), meaning \(\mathbf{B} \approx \mathbf{0}\). Group means \(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_l\) are all close to \(\bar{\mathbf{x}}\). Fail to reject \(H_0: \boldsymbol{\tau}_1 = \cdots = \boldsymbol{\tau}_g = \mathbf{0}\).
When \(\Lambda^* \approx 0\)
\(|\mathbf{W}| \ll |\mathbf{W}+\mathbf{B}|\), meaning \(\mathbf{B}\) is large relative to \(\mathbf{W}\). Groups explain most of the total variation. Reject \(H_0: \boldsymbol{\tau}_1 = \cdots = \boldsymbol{\tau}_g = \mathbf{0}\).
Link to LRT
Wilks' \(\Lambda^*\) is a likelihood ratio test statistic — the third form \(|\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_0|/|\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}|\) makes this explicit. It is directly related to Hotelling's \(T^2\): for \(g=2\) groups, \(\Lambda^*\) reduces to a scaled \(T^2\). The eigenvalue form links it to Roy's largest root \(\theta = \lambda_1\), Pillai's trace \(V^{(s)} = \sum \lambda_i/(1+\lambda_i)\), and the Lawley-Hotelling trace \(U^{(s)} = \sum \lambda_i\) — all of which use the same eigenvalues \(\lambda_i\) of \(\mathbf{W}^{-1}\mathbf{B}\), the multivariate analogue of \(B/W\).
Exact and Approximate Distributions

The distribution of \(\Lambda^*\) depends on \(p\) and \(g\). For a few special cases we have exact \(F\)-distributions:

Variables \(p\) Groups \(g\) Scaling factor Distribution
\(p=1\) \(g \geq 2\) \(\dfrac{\sum n_l - g}{g-1}\cdot\dfrac{1-\Lambda^*}{\Lambda^*}\) \(F_{g-1,\,\sum n_l - g}\)
\(p=2\) \(g \geq 2\) \(\dfrac{\sum n_l - g - 1}{g-1}\cdot\dfrac{1-\sqrt{\Lambda^*}}{\sqrt{\Lambda^*}}\) \(F_{2(g-1),\,2(\sum n_l-g-1)}\)
\(p \geq 1\) \(g=2\) \(\dfrac{\sum n_l - p - 1}{p}\cdot\dfrac{1-\Lambda^*}{\Lambda^*}\) \(F_{p,\,\sum n_l - p - 1}\)
\(p \geq 1\) \(g=3\) \(\dfrac{\sum n_l - p - 2}{p}\cdot\dfrac{1-\sqrt{\Lambda^*}}{\sqrt{\Lambda^*}}\) \(F_{2p,\,2(\sum n_l-p-2)}\)

For large \(n\) and cases not covered above, use the \(\chi^2\) approximation:

Large-sample approximation
\[-\!\left(n - 1 - \frac{p+g}{2}\right)\ln\Lambda^* \;\dot{\sim}\; \chi^2_{p(g-1)}\] The correction factor \(\left(n-1-\frac{p+g}{2}\right)\) improves on the naive \(-2\ln\Lambda\) for finite samples.
Box's M Test — Checking Equal Covariances

Before running MANOVA, test the assumption \(H_0: \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_1 = \cdots = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_g\) using Box's M. This is also an LRT — instead of comparing means, we compare individual group covariance matrices \(S_i\) against the pooled estimate \(S_p\):

\[M = \left(\sum_i(n_i-1)\right)\ln|S_p| - \sum_i(n_i-1)\ln|S_i|\] Scaled \(M \sim \chi^2_{p(p+1)(g-1)/2}\) approximately.

Note: Box's M is sensitive to non-normality, so a significant result does not automatically invalidate MANOVA — consider the context before abandoning the analysis.

4
Post-Rejection Analysis

Rejecting \(H_0: \boldsymbol{\mu}_1 = \cdots = \boldsymbol{\mu}_g\) tells us something is different — but not what or where. We then zoom in using two complementary approaches.

Step 1 — Univariate ANOVAs: Which Variable Differs?

Run \(p\) separate ANOVA \(F\)-tests, one per variable. Apply a Bonferroni correction, testing each at level \(\alpha/p\) to control the overall error rate.

Example
MANOVA rejects equal mean vectors across fertiliser groups. Running \(p=2\) ANOVAs at level \(\alpha/2\):
  • Yield: \(F = 1.2\), \(p\text{-value} = 0.31\) — not significant
  • Height: \(F = 8.7\), \(p\text{-value} = 0.001\) — significant
Conclusion: it is plant height, not yield, that is driving the difference in mean vectors.
Step 2 — Bonferroni Intervals: Which Groups Differ?

For each variable \(i\) that was significant, and each pair of groups \((k, l)\), construct:

Bonferroni Confidence Interval for \(\tau_{ki} - \tau_{li}\)
\[\bar{x}_{ki} - \bar{x}_{li} \;\pm\; t_{n-g}\!\left(\frac{\alpha}{pg(g-1)}\right)\sqrt{\frac{w_{ii}}{n-g}\left(\frac{1}{n_k}+\frac{1}{n_l}\right)}\] with \(m = \frac{pg(g-1)}{2}\) simultaneous intervals total.

The rule is simple: if the interval straddles zero, those groups are not significantly different for that variable. If the interval is entirely positive or entirely negative, reject equality for that pair.

Example — Height intervals (\(g=3\) groups, \(\frac{g(g-1)}{2}=3\) pairs)
← negative       0       positive →
Groups \(1\) vs \(2\) (height)
FAIL TO REJECT
Groups \(1\) vs \(3\) (height)
REJECT
Groups \(2\) vs \(3\) (height)
FAIL TO REJECT
Fertiliser groups 1 and 3 produce significantly different mean plant heights. Groups 1 vs 2 and 2 vs 3 are not significantly different.
What each step answers
Univariate ANOVAs answer: which of the \(p\) variables differ across groups?
Bonferroni intervals answer: which specific group pairs \((k,l)\) differ for those variables?
Together: "Plant height (\(i=2\)) differs significantly between fertiliser groups \(1\) and \(3\) (\(k=1, l=3\))."
5
Two-Way ANOVA & MANOVA

We now classify observations by two factors simultaneously. In our example: Factor 1 = fertiliser type (\(g=3\) levels), Factor 2 = irrigation level (\(b=2\) levels), giving \(g \times b = 6\) combinations with \(n=5\) observations each, and \(p=2\) response variables (yield, height).

The Two-Way Model
Two-Way MANOVA Model
\[\mathbf{X}_{lkr} = \boldsymbol{\mu} + \boldsymbol{\tau}_l + \boldsymbol{\beta}_k + \boldsymbol{\gamma}_{lk} + \mathbf{e}_{lkr}\]
  • \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) — overall mean vector
  • \(\boldsymbol{\tau}_l\) — effect of fertiliser level \(l\)
  • \(\boldsymbol{\beta}_k\) — effect of irrigation level \(k\)
  • \(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{lk}\) — interaction between fertiliser \(l\) and irrigation \(k\)
  • \(\mathbf{e}_{lkr} \sim N_p(\mathbf{0}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma})\)
The Variance Decomposition
Total \((X_{lkr} - \bar{x})\)
=
Factor 1 (fertiliser) \((\bar{x}_{l\cdot} - \bar{x})\)
+
Factor 2 (irrigation) \((\bar{x}_{\cdot k} - \bar{x})\)
+
Interaction \((\bar{x}_{lk} - \bar{x}_{l\cdot} - \bar{x}_{\cdot k} + \bar{x})\)
+
Residual \((X_{lkr} - \bar{x}_{lk})\)
SourceInterpretationdf
Factor 1 How far each fertiliser group mean \(\bar{x}_{l\cdot}\) is from the overall mean \(\bar{x}\) \(g-1\)
Factor 2 How far each irrigation group mean \(\bar{x}_{\cdot k}\) is from the overall mean \(\bar{x}\) \(b-1\)
Interaction How far each cell mean \(\bar{x}_{lk}\) is from what we'd predict from the two factor effects alone — i.e. the extra effect of being in a specific combination \((g-1)(b-1)\)
Residual How far each observation is from its own cell mean \(\bar{x}_{lk}\) \(gb(n-1)\)
Total How far each observation is from the overall mean \(gbn-1\)
Why interaction df = \((g-1)(b-1)\)?
You start with \(gb\) cell means. After accounting for \(g-1\) factor 1 effects, \(b-1\) factor 2 effects, and 1 overall mean, you have \(gb - (g-1) - (b-1) - 1 = (g-1)(b-1)\) free interaction parameters remaining.
Understanding Interaction

The interaction term answers: does the effect of fertiliser depend on irrigation level? If the two factors act purely additively, the interaction terms are all zero. The expected cell mean under no interaction would be:

\[\text{Expected } \bar{x}_{lk} = \bar{x} + (\bar{x}_{l\cdot} - \bar{x}) + (\bar{x}_{\cdot k} - \bar{x}) = \bar{x}_{l\cdot} + \bar{x}_{\cdot k} - \bar{x}\] So interaction \(= \bar{x}_{lk} - \bar{x}_{l\cdot} - \bar{x}_{\cdot k} + \bar{x}\): the gap between the actual and the predicted cell mean.
Interaction Example — Yield

Without interaction: high fertiliser always adds 10 units of yield regardless of irrigation.

With interaction: high fertiliser adds 10 units under high irrigation, but only 2 units under low irrigation — the fertiliser effect depends on the irrigation level.

Interaction Plots — Reading the Visual

Interaction plots show group means connected across factor 1 levels, with separate lines for each level of factor 2. The key rule:

Parallel lines \(\Rightarrow\) No interaction
The effect of factor 1 is the same at both levels of factor 2. Factors act independently and additively. The \(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{lk} \approx \mathbf{0}\). Main effects \(\boldsymbol{\tau}_l\) and \(\boldsymbol{\beta}_k\) can be interpreted cleanly.
Crossing / diverging lines \(\Rightarrow\) Interaction present
The effect of factor 1 changes depending on the level of factor 2. Some \(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{lk} \neq \mathbf{0}\). Main effects \(\boldsymbol{\tau}_l\) and \(\boldsymbol{\beta}_k\) cannot be interpreted in isolation.
Interaction Plots — Yield and Height across Fertiliser \(\times\) Irrigation
Yield — no interaction (parallel lines, \(\gamma_{lk}\approx 0\))
Height — mild interaction (diverging lines)
Hypothetical — strong interaction (crossing lines)

6
The Full Decision Process
Two-Way MANOVA — Complete Testing Workflow
1
Test interaction using \(\Lambda^*_{int}\). Always do this first.
Example: Is the effect of fertiliser (\(\boldsymbol{\tau}_l\)) on our \(p=2\) responses different at the \(b=2\) irrigation levels?
✓ Significant
Some \(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{lk} \neq \mathbf{0}\). Factor effects have no clean interpretation. Drop to \(p\) separate univariate two-way ANOVAs to identify which variables show interaction. Use interaction plots.
✗ Not significant
All \(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{lk} \approx \mathbf{0}\). Factors act independently. Proceed to test main effects \(\boldsymbol{\tau}_l\) and \(\boldsymbol{\beta}_k\).
↓
2
Test Factor 1 and Factor 2 main effects using \(\Lambda^*_{fac1}\) and \(\Lambda^*_{fac2}\).
Example: Does fertiliser type significantly affect \(\mathbf{X} = (\text{yield}, \text{height})'\)? Does irrigation?
↓
3
Univariate ANOVAs for each significant factor — one per variable \(i = 1,\ldots,p\).
Example: Fertiliser is significant. Running \(p=2\) ANOVAs shows height (\(i=2\)) is significant but yield (\(i=1\)) is not.
↓
4
Bonferroni confidence intervals for each significant variable — all \(\frac{g(g-1)}{2}\) group pairs.
Example: For height, test pairs \((1,2),\ (1,3),\ (2,3)\). Find that only fertiliser groups \(1\) and \(3\) differ significantly (\(\tau_{1,2} - \tau_{3,2} \neq 0\)).
Final Conclusion — Our Example
Final Conclusion — Our Example
There is no significant interaction between fertiliser type and irrigation level (\(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{lk} \approx \mathbf{0}\)). Fertiliser type has a significant effect on the mean response vector \(\boldsymbol{\mu}_l\), driven specifically by plant height (\(i=2\), not yield). Among the \(g=3\) fertiliser groups, it is specifically groups \(1\) and \(3\) whose mean heights differ significantly from each other.
