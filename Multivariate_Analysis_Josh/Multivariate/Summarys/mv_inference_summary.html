Multivariate Inference

Hotelling's T², Wilks' Λ, invariance, confidence regions and intervals
01

Hotelling's T² statistic

definition One-sample test of the mean vector
$H_0: \boldsymbol{\mu} = \boldsymbol{\mu}_0 \quad \text{vs} \quad H_1: \boldsymbol{\mu} \neq \boldsymbol{\mu}_0, \quad \mathbf{X}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{X}_n \sim N_p(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma})$
$$T^2 = n(\bar{\mathbf{x}} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_0)^\prime \mathbf{S}^{-1}(\bar{\mathbf{x}} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_0) = n \cdot D^2(\bar{\mathbf{x}}, \boldsymbol{\mu}_0)$$
$$T^2 \sim \frac{(n-1)p}{n-p}\,F_{p,\,n-p} \qquad \text{Reject } H_0 \text{ if } T^2 > \frac{(n-1)p}{n-p}\,F_{p,\,n-p}(\alpha)$$
Sweat example: $n=20$, $p=3$, $\boldsymbol{\mu}_0 = (4, 50, 10)^\prime$. Computed $T^2 = 9.74$, $p\text{-value} = 0.065$. Fail to reject at 5% — the hypothesised mean is plausible.
Key idea
$T^2$ measures the squared Mahalanobis distance between $\bar{\mathbf{x}}$ and $\boldsymbol{\mu}_0$, scaled by $n$. It is the multivariate analogue of $t^2 = n(\bar{x} - \mu_0)^2/s^2$. The $\mathbf{S}^{-1}$ term accounts for variance and correlation — distances in high-variance directions are down-weighted.
derivation Why $T^2 \sim F$ and not $\chi^2$
$$T^2 = N_p(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})^\prime \left[\frac{1}{n-1}W_{p,n-1}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\right]^{-1} N_p(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}) = \frac{\chi^2_p}{\chi^2_{n-1}/(n-1)}$$

$\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ is unknown and estimated by $\mathbf{S}$. The Wishart matrix $(n-1)\mathbf{S} \sim W_{p,n-1}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})$ plays the role of $\chi^2_{n-1}$ in the denominator of the univariate $F$. As $n \to \infty$, $\mathbf{S} \to \boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ and $T^2 \xrightarrow{d} \chi^2_p$.

$$\frac{n-p}{(n-1)p}\,T^2 \sim F_{p,\,n-p} \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad \text{p-value} = 1 - \texttt{pf}\!\left(\frac{n-p}{(n-1)p}T^2,\; p,\; n-p\right)$$
repeated measures Testing equality of means across $q$ time points
$H_0: \boldsymbol{\mu}_1 = \boldsymbol{\mu}_2 = \cdots = \boldsymbol{\mu}_q \iff H_0: \mathbf{C}\boldsymbol{\mu} = \mathbf{0}$
$$\mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{X} \sim N_{q-1}(\mathbf{C}\boldsymbol{\mu},\, \mathbf{C}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{C}^\prime)$$
$$T^2 = n(\mathbf{C}\bar{\mathbf{x}})^\prime(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{C}^\prime)^{-1}(\mathbf{C}\bar{\mathbf{x}}) \sim \frac{(n-1)(q-1)}{n-q+1}\,F_{q-1,\,n-q+1}$$
The contrast matrix $\mathbf{C}$ encodes differences between time points. Two common choices: all vs first ($\mu_1 - \mu_i$) or successive ($\mu_i - \mu_{i+1}$). Both give the same test decision.
02

Invariance of $T^2$

theorem $T^2$ is invariant under linear transformations
Let $\mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{X} + \mathbf{d}$ where $\mathbf{C}_{p\times p}$ is non-singular and $\mathbf{d}_{p\times 1}$ is a constant vector.
$$T^2_Y = n(\bar{\mathbf{y}} - \mathbf{C}\boldsymbol{\mu}_0 - \mathbf{d})^\prime \mathbf{S}_Y^{-1}(\bar{\mathbf{y}} - \mathbf{C}\boldsymbol{\mu}_0 - \mathbf{d}) = T^2_X$$

Proof sketch: since $\bar{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{C}\bar{\mathbf{x}} + \mathbf{d}$ and $\mathbf{S}_Y = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{S}_X\mathbf{C}^\prime$,

$$T^2_Y = n[\mathbf{C}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_0)]^\prime (\mathbf{C}\mathbf{S}_X\mathbf{C}^\prime)^{-1}[\mathbf{C}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_0)] = n(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_0)^\prime \mathbf{C}^\prime(\mathbf{C}^\prime)^{-1}\mathbf{S}_X^{-1}\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{C}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_0) = T^2_X$$
Implication
Changing units of measurement (e.g. cm to inches), re-centering, or rotating the data does not change the test statistic or its conclusion. The test is about the geometry of the data, not the coordinate system.
03

Likelihood ratio test and Wilks' $\Lambda$

LRT General likelihood ratio framework
$$\Lambda = \frac{\max_{\theta \in \Theta_0} L(\theta)}{\max_{\theta \in \Theta} L(\theta)} \in [0,1] \qquad \text{Reject } H_0 \text{ if } \Lambda < c_\alpha$$
$$-2\log(\Lambda) \xrightarrow{d} \chi^2_{\nu - \nu_0} \quad \text{as } n \to \infty, \quad \nu - \nu_0 = \dim(\Theta) - \dim(\Theta_0)$$
Intuition: $\Lambda \approx 1$ means restricting to $H_0$ barely reduces the likelihood — plausible. $\Lambda \approx 0$ means the restricted model is far worse — implausible. This is a relative likelihood comparison, analogous to a difference in deviances.
derivation Restricted MLE under $H_0: \boldsymbol{\mu} = \boldsymbol{\mu}_0$

Maximising $L(\boldsymbol{\mu}_0, \boldsymbol{\Sigma})$ over $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ only (since $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ is fixed at $\boldsymbol{\mu}_0$):

$$\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_0 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n(\mathbf{x}_j - \boldsymbol{\mu}_0)(\mathbf{x}_j - \boldsymbol{\mu}_0)^\prime = \underbrace{\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{\text{unrestricted MLE}} + \underbrace{(\bar{\mathbf{x}} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_0)(\bar{\mathbf{x}} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_0)^\prime}_{\text{does not vanish since }\boldsymbol{\mu}_0 \neq \bar{\mathbf{x}}}$$
Why the extra term doesn't vanish
In the unrestricted MLE, $\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} = \bar{\mathbf{x}}$ is chosen to minimise residuals — so $(\bar{\mathbf{x}} - \hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}) = \mathbf{0}$ exactly. Under $H_0$, $\boldsymbol{\mu}_0$ is fixed and generally $\boldsymbol{\mu}_0 \neq \bar{\mathbf{x}}$, so the cross-product term remains.
Wilks' $\Lambda$ The LRT statistic for the mean vector test
$$\Lambda^{2/n} = \frac{|\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}|}{|\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_0|} = \frac{|\mathbf{A}|}{|\mathbf{A} + n(\bar{\mathbf{x}} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_0)(\bar{\mathbf{x}} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_0)^\prime|}$$

The $\frac{1}{n}$'s cancel since $|\alpha \mathbf{A}| = \alpha^p|\mathbf{A}|$ pulls $\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)^p$ from both numerator and denominator, leaving an $n$ in front of the outer product in the denominator.

$$\Lambda^{2/n} = \left(1 + \frac{T^2}{n-1}\right)^{-1} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad T^2 = (n-1)\!\left(\frac{1}{\Lambda^{2/n}} - 1\right)$$
Equivalence
The $T^2$ test and the LRT are identical for testing $H_0: \boldsymbol{\mu} = \boldsymbol{\mu}_0$. They always give the same decision. Use $T^2$ when testing means (exact, finite-sample $F$ distribution); use LRT when testing $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ or any parameter where no purpose-built exact test exists (relies on large-sample $\chi^2$ approximation).

Confidence ellipsoid for $\boldsymbol{\mu}$

confidence region $100(1-\alpha)\%$ ellipsoid for the mean vector
$$\left\{\boldsymbol{\mu} : n(\bar{\mathbf{x}} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\prime \mathbf{S}^{-1}(\bar{\mathbf{x}} - \boldsymbol{\mu}) \leq \frac{(n-1)p}{n-p}\,F_{p,\,n-p}(\alpha)\right\}$$

The ellipsoid is centred at $\bar{\mathbf{x}}$ and its axes come from the eigendecomposition $\mathbf{S}\mathbf{e}_i = \lambda_i\mathbf{e}_i$:

$$\text{Axes: } \pm\underbrace{\sqrt{\lambda_i}}_{\text{relative length}}\underbrace{\sqrt{\frac{p(n-1)}{n(n-p)}\,F_{p,n-p}(\alpha)}}_{c \text{ — critical value scaling}}\,\mathbf{e}_i$$
Frequentist interpretation
$\boldsymbol{\mu}$ is fixed. The data, $\bar{\mathbf{x}}$ and $\mathbf{S}$ are random — so the ellipsoid shifts and rotates every resample. Across many resamples, 95% of those ellipsoids contain the fixed true $\boldsymbol{\mu}$. The 5% that miss do so because $\bar{\mathbf{x}}$ was pulled too far from $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ by an unrepresentative sample.
scaling How $c$ changes with sample size
$$c_{\text{small}\,n} = \sqrt{\frac{p(n-1)}{n(n-p)}\,F_{p,n-p}(\alpha)}$$

Exact under MVN for any $n$. Extra factor accounts for randomness in $\mathbf{S}$.

$$c_{\text{large}\,n} = \sqrt{\chi^2_p(\alpha)}$$

Valid as $n \to \infty$ since $\mathbf{S} \to \boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ and $\frac{p(n-1)}{n(n-p)}F_{p,n-p}(\alpha) \to \chi^2_p(\alpha)$.

Three approaches to confidence intervals for $\boldsymbol{\mu}$

Width ordering (narrowest to widest):

$T^2$ simultaneous
individual $t$
One-at-a-time $t$-intervals
$$\bar{x}_i \pm t_{n-1}\!\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\sqrt{\frac{s_{ii}}{n}}$$

Each interval has coverage $1-\alpha$ individually. Joint coverage drops to $(1-\alpha)^p < 1-\alpha$.

Use when: making a single pre-specified comparison. Do not use for simultaneous inference on all $p$ means.
Bonferroni
Bonferroni intervals
$$\bar{x}_i \pm t_{n-1}\!\left(\frac{\alpha}{2m}\right)\sqrt{\frac{s_{ii}}{n}}$$

where $m$ is the number of intervals. Guaranteed joint coverage $\geq 1-\alpha$ by the Bonferroni inequality: $\Pr(\text{all hold}) \geq 1 - \sum_{i=1}^m \alpha_i$.

Use when: making $m$ pre-specified comparisons. Narrower than $T^2$ when $m$ is small. Preferred in practice for $p$ means.
$T^2$ simultaneous
$T^2$ simultaneous intervals
$$\bar{x}_i \pm \sqrt{\frac{p(n-1)}{n-p}\,F_{p,n-p}(\alpha)}\sqrt{\frac{s_{ii}}{n}}$$

Valid simultaneously for all linear combinations $\mathbf{a}^\prime\boldsymbol{\mu}$. Guaranteed coverage $1-\alpha$ jointly.

Use when: you want coverage for any possible linear combination, not just the $p$ means. Widest — necessary when exploring data-driven contrasts.
large sample $\chi^2$ approximation for large $n$ (no normality assumed)
$$\bar{x}_i \pm \sqrt{\chi^2_p(\alpha)}\sqrt{\frac{s_{ii}}{n}}$$

Valid by the CLT as $n \to \infty$ even without MVN. Replace $F$ critical value with $\chi^2_p(\alpha)$ since $\frac{p(n-1)}{n(n-p)}F_{p,n-p}(\alpha) \to \chi^2_p(\alpha)$ as $n \to \infty$ and $\mathbf{S} \to \boldsymbol{\Sigma}$.

Bonferroni inequality Why Bonferroni works
$$\Pr(\text{all } C_i \text{ true}) \geq 1 - \sum_{i=1}^m \Pr(C_i \text{ false}) = 1 - \sum_{i=1}^m \alpha_i$$

Setting $\alpha_i = \alpha/m$ for each interval and using $t_{n-1}(\alpha/2m)$ as the critical value ensures joint coverage $\geq 1 - \alpha$.

Example ($m=6$, $1-\alpha=0.95$, $n=15$, $p=4$): $T^2$ multiplier $= \sqrt{\frac{4\times14}{11}\times3.36} = 4.14$ vs individual $t_{14}(0.025) = 2.145$ vs Bonferroni $t_{14}(0.05/12) \approx 3.00$. Bonferroni sits between the two extremes.
06

Key convergence results as $n \to \infty$

$$\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}} = \frac{n-1}{n}\mathbf{S} \xrightarrow{n\to\infty} \mathbf{S} \xrightarrow{n\to\infty} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}$$
$$T^2 \sim \frac{(n-1)p}{n-p}F_{p,n-p} \xrightarrow{d} \chi^2_p \quad \text{as } n \to \infty$$
$$-2\log(\Lambda) \xrightarrow{d} \chi^2_p \quad \text{as } n \to \infty$$

Both $T^2$ and the LRT converge to $\chi^2_p$ for large $n$, driven by two simultaneous effects: (1) the CLT making $\bar{\mathbf{x}}$ approximately normal, and (2) $\mathbf{S}^{-1} \to \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}$ eliminating the extra uncertainty that caused the $F$ distribution in finite samples.
